Part 1 — Scope and Product Description

1.1 What This Document Is

This document is the engineering and production specification for the BGF-28 transparent ceramic optical element, Tier 1 baseline product. It contains every specification, process step, acceptance criterion, and operating instruction that an engineering team needs to build, qualify, mount, and operate the element.

1.2 What the Element Is

The BGF-28 is a transparent polycrystalline ceramic lens element of nominal composition Ba0.72Gd0.28F2.28, in the single-phase cubic fluorite structure, with mean grain size ≤ 145 nm and clean grain boundaries. It is supplied as a polished, coated disc of 12 mm outside diameter (10 mm clear aperture, 1 mm mounting zone per side; the mounting zone carries the hermetic ALD coating but not the optical AR/UV coating). Mounting in a barrel is optional (see § 1.7).

The element's optical constants are nd = 1.505 ± 0.001 and Vd = 62 ± 1. It has an approximate spectral extent from 300 nm to 8 µm, but the specified in-line transmission is T(1–5 µm) ≥ 0.85; transmission from 5–8 µm is not qualified in this Tier 1 specification. The clear aperture has anti-reflection coating. The element is macroscopically isotropic (no preferred crystal orientation) and has high thermal conductivity. It is not hygroscopic under the specified hermetic coating, and it is mechanically stable over the operating envelope.

1.3 What the Element Replaces

The BGF-28 is a drop-in replacement for crown-class optical elements in existing lens systems and instruments:

  • N-SK16 and N-BK7 glass elements — replaced on the axes of bandwidth (glass transmits to ~2 µm; BGF-28 has specified T ≥ 0.85 through 5 µm, with an approximate but unqualified extent to 8 µm), thermal conductivity (BGF-28 conducts heat approximately 10× better than glass, reducing thermal lensing), and cost (BGF-28 element cost is comparable to or lower than a glass asphere).
  • Fused silica elements — replaced on the axes of bandwidth (silica stops at 2.5 µm; BGF-28 has specified transmission through 5 µm) and cost.
  • CaF2 and BaF2 single-crystal elements — replaced on the axes of cost (crystal elements cost 1.5–4× more), isotropy (the ceramic has no crystal-axis stress birefringence), and ruggedness (the ceramic does not cleave).

1.4 What the Element Does Not Replace

The BGF-28 is not a replacement for:

  • A lightweight glass element in a weight-sensitive system. The element is approximately 1.85× heavier per unit optical power than N-SK16 glass (density 5.44 g/cm³ vs. 3.60 g/cm³ at comparable index range). Weight-sensitive instruments should use a polymer or glass element where weight dominates.
  • A self-athermal element. The element has a low thermo-optic coefficient (|dn/dT| ≈ 1×10⁻⁵/K, about 10× lower than polymer) but is not self-athermal. System athermalization is required: autofocus for consumer-class instruments, or a PBT-GF30 barrel (CTE 35 ± 2 ppm/K) for fixed-focus at f/4 and slower across the stated element and barrel tolerance windows. Fixed-focus at f/2.8 requires individual thermal qualification of the element–barrel assembly; matching solely within the stated barrel CTE window cannot guarantee every allowed element meets the stated depth-of-focus criterion. For fixed-focus at f/2 and faster, active autofocus is required (see Part 8).
  • A complete lens system. The element is one optical component. The full dispersion curve required for chromatic balancing of a multi-element system is not part of this product; only the two-term dispersion consistent with the stated nd and Vd is provided (see § 8.7).
  • A high-power laser optic. The laser-induced damage threshold of the ceramic is not qualified in this Tier 1 product. High-power laser use is a higher tier offering (see Part 13).
  • A deep-UV optic below 300 nm. Transmission falls below 50% at 300 nm in the standard product (UV-transmitting variant: see § 1.6). Deep-UV below 300 nm is a higher tier offering.

2.3 Safety Data Summary

The finished element is non-toxic as a solid. The fluoride material is slightly hygroscopic; the hermetic ALD coating and edge seal on the finished element protect it from moisture ingress under the operating envelope (Part 8 § 8.1). Health and safety requirements apply to the production stages (powder, HF, fluorine gas) and are specified in Part 12. Part 3 — Powder Production

3.1 Overview

The powder production line converts high-purity Ba and Gd precursors into a homogeneous, single-phase, sub-micron barium gadolinium fluoride powder with controlled stoichiometry. The line comprises eleven steps. Every step is specified below with its parameters, its critical controls, and its acceptance criteria.

The line must be operated free of steel contact from the mill stage onward. No iron, nickel, or cobalt alloy may contact the slurry, the powder, or the green body. This is a hard requirement: a single steel contact point is sufficient to contaminate a batch above the Fe spec.

3.3 Process Flow

3.3.1 Step 1 — Precursor QC

Every incoming lot of Gd2O3, BaCO3/Ba(NO3)2, and NH4F is sampled and analyzed by ICP-MS for the critical impurities listed in § 3.2. No lot enters the line until its ICP-MS result is approved. Store all precursors in a dry glovebox (< 1% RH) after opening.

3.3.2 Step 2 — Co-precipitation (reverse addition)

Reverse addition is mandatory. The metal solution is added to a fluoride excess; the reverse (fluoride added to metal) is prohibited. The reason is the 11-order-of-magnitude difference between Ksp(BaF2) ≈ 1.8 × 10−7 and Ksp(GdF3) ≈ 1.8 × 10−19. Forward addition produces a GdF3 core with a BaF2 shell (inhomogeneous powder, striae after sintering). Reverse addition, into a fluoride excess, precipitates both cations simultaneously and produces a mixed fluoride.

  • No steel contact: all vessels, tubing, stirrers, filters, mill jars, and drying trays in PTFE, PFA, or high-purity polymer.
  • Dry atmosphere: all powder handling after calcination is in a glovebox at < 10% RH (preferably < 1% for calcined powder).

  • Traceability: every powder lot carries a unique lot number from precursor receipt through release. Retain a sample of each lot for 2 years.

4.1 Overview

The powder is consolidated into a dense, transparent body by a three-step thermal route: cold isostatic pressing (CIP), pressureless sintering in a fluorine-maintaining atmosphere, and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) to remove the last porosity. A stress-relief anneal follows, and the body is cooled slowly to avoid freezing fluorine-vacancy defects. Each step is specified below.

4.5 Step 15 — Hot Isostatic Pressing (HIP)

Mo is used as the sample-contacting surface because it is 2.4× more inert than Ta against the fluoride (ΔG(MoF6) ≈ +1964 kJ/mol for the capsule reaction vs. +820 kJ/mol for Ta) and because the Mo liner can be removed chemically (HNO3/H2O2) if the sample sticks; the Ta capsule cannot be removed chemically without attacking the part.

Part 6 — Coating

6.1 Overview

The coating stack provides three functions at once: (i) mechanical protection and chemical hermeticity (adhesion layer + outer hard layer), (ii) anti-reflection over the visible and near-IR band, and (iii) UV blocking in the standard variant (a UV mirror above 300 nm). The two variants (VIS/UVBLOCK and UVTRANSMIT) share the adhesion and hard layers and differ only in the presence of the UV mirror.

Part 7 — Mounting and Assembly

7.1 Overview

The element is mounted in a two-part barrel: an outer barrel that sets the lens-to-sensor distance and provides the athermalization correction, and an inner ring that holds the ceramic element. The element is held axially by a hard machined shoulder and a Belleville-washer-loaded retainer; it is centered radially by a thin silicone ring. The mounting is designed so that the ceramic is never in tension in service and so that the silicone does not creep into the axial optical path.

7.3 Assembly Sequence

  1. Sub-assembly cleaning. Clean the SS316 inner ring, retainer, and barrel in an ultrasonic bath (IPA, 5 min). Dry in N2. All parts handled in an ISO Class 7 cleanroom.
  2. RTV application. Apply platinum-cure silicone RTV to the inner ring in a 0.2 mm-thick annular bead at the mounting zone location. The bead is recessed behind a lip on the inner ring so it is not in line-of-sight to the clear aperture.
  3. Element placement. Place the ceramic element on the axial hard stop of the inner ring. Verify it seats fully; no rocking.
  4. RTV cure. Cure at room temperature for 24 h (or 60 °C for 2 h, then room temperature 12 h). Do not cure at ≥ 100 °C (the RTV would be cured while the SS/ceramic CTE mismatch loads the bond).
  5. Retainer installation. Install the SS316 retainer with a Belleville washer between the retainer and the element. Torque to 0.1 N·m. Apply Loctite 222 (low-strength thread-locker) to the retainer threads before torquing. Install a safety wire through the retainer and the inner ring.
  6. Barrel assembly. Install the inner ring sub-assembly into the outer PBT-GF30 barrel. The interface is a slip fit with a thin compliant pad (0.1 mm EPDM) that decouples the CTE mismatch between the two parts.
  7. Alignment. Set the lens-to-sensor distance per the instrument optical design. Lock with an M3 × 0.5 flat-point barrel set screw with a pre-applied nylon locking patch. Its threads are in the PBT-GF30 outer barrel and its tip bears on the SS316 inner ring. Torque the screw to 0.1 N·m and install a safety wire between the screw head and a suitable barrel anchor. Do not apply liquid anaerobic thread-locker to the PBT-GF30 threads. Verify the residual focus shift against the thermal model (see § 8.6); assemblies intended for fixed-focus f/2.8 require individual thermal qualification.
  8. Final inspection. Visual: no debris on the clear aperture. Functional: transmission at 500 nm (per Part 6). Mechanical: retainer and barrel set-screw installation torques recorded (0.1 N·m ± 10% each); safety wires intact; no RTV visible on the clear aperture. Mark the assembly with the batch code.

7.4 Handling and Cleanliness at Assembly

  • All assembly steps in ISO Class 7 cleanroom (or better) or in a laminar-flow hood.
  • Cleanroom garments (coverall, hood, booties, nitrile gloves) for all personnel.
  • No ester, mineral, or silicone oils in the assembly area. All lubricants must be PFPE (perfluoropolyether).
  • No acid-cure silicone RTV anywhere in the assembly area (acetic-acid cure would attack the fluoride).
  • Nitrogen purge of the clean hood before mounting the element.

Part 10 — Proof Test and Reliability

10.1 Proof Test

Every element is proof-tested before shipment. The nominal target is 25 MPa peak biaxial tensile stress. For a simply supported circular plate with effective support radius a = 5.0 mm, thickness t = 2.0 mm, and Poisson's ratio ν = 0.25, a pressure differential of 3.3 MPa gives a calculated central stress of approximately 25 MPa. The effective support radius must be fixed and verified by the proof-fixture design; the phrase “O-ring at the mounting zone” alone is insufficient because the zone extends from r = 5.0 to 6.0 mm. The fixture must also account for O-ring contact width, actual part thickness, and departures from the assumed support condition before the nominal pressure is treated as a 25 MPa proof. Survivors are accepted; failed elements are scrapped.

At 25 MPa nominal proof stress, calculated survival is approximately 99.91%. The corresponding nominal service limit is 12.5 MPa, above the stated mount and thermal loads (< 5 MPa in service). This comparison applies only if the proof stress is achieved in the qualified fixture.

11.3 Total Cost of Ownership

Over the life of the instrument, BGF-28 is expected to cost less than CaF2 not only in purchase price but also in service life: the ceramic is not cleavable, does not require crystal-axis alignment, and does not suffer from stress birefringence. The 10-year service-life probability (> 99.99%) and the ready availability of replacement elements reduce the instrument's long-term maintenance cost.
